A Bug-Eyed Fish Named Charles

What a small-town weekly noticed about Shawn Fitzpatrick, born in January 1959, and about the large and busy family around him.

Weeping Water · St. Joseph · Omaha1951 to 199192 clippings

A country weekly in the 1960s printed nearly everything. It printed who drove to Lincoln on Sunday, whose rose ramblers were in bloom, and which first grader brought cookies. Most of the people in it never did anything the wider world would call news, and that is what makes it such a good record of ordinary lives.

Shawn Fitzpatrick turns up in the Weeping Water Republican at his birth and keeps turning up: at birthday parties, in the school columns, in a Cub Scout den his mother ran, and in one line of the town's “Around Town” column, between a Holstein that had triplets and a note of congratulations. In the summer of 1966, the paper reported, he had “a new bug-eyed fish named Charles Fishpatrick.”

Around him is a family the paper followed just as closely: a mother who published poems on the front page, a grandfather who painted, a brother in Vietnam, a sister with a teddy bear in a state art show, and cousins who came for an “indefinite visit.” Click any clipping to read the corrected transcription and open the original page in the Weeping Water Public Library's archive.

A note on names

The paper had limited time for fact-checking, and these spellings all refer to the same people. Sheila is often “Shelia.” Denis is almost always “Dennis.” Neil is sometimes A. Neil or, in full, Arthur Neil. Violet is “Vi” or “Mrs. Art Fitzpatrick.” Harrison Hartley appears as H. S. and once as J. S. Hartley. Marian Martin is also Maryon and Marion, Debbie is Debby or Debra, and Rodney is Roddie. Transcriptions keep whatever the paper printed.

1959–1964Arthur Shawn

Balloons, Cake and Jello

He arrived at two in the morning and made the front page by Thursday.

Neil and Mary Fitzpatrick's fifth child was born on Monday, January 19, 1959, at St. Mary's Hospital in Nebraska City, the same hospital where his brother Denis had been born less than two years before. The paper reported that he had been named Arthur Shawn. He carried the first name of his grandfather Art Fitzpatrick, who lived in town, and of his father, whose full name was Arthur Neil. He has been Shawn in the paper ever since.

Then come the birthdays, which fell conveniently in the slow news weeks of January. At three he had 15 guests, who “spent the time playing games with balloons.” At four his sister Sheila, about nine years old, “planned many interesting games.” At seven it was eight children on a Wednesday, and the first grade got cookies too.

“His 15 guests spent the time playing games with balloons.”Weeping Water Republican, January 25, 1962

1964–1970School news

Book Worms and a Combat Helmet

Each classroom sent its own column to the paper, so a whole childhood is written down by the children's teachers and classmates.

Shawn entered kindergarten in the fall of 1964 in a class of 33, listed by name with a parent or guardian beside each child. That list is one of the few places he is “Shawn Arthur.” By first grade he was in the columns for bringing cookies, alongside Fonda Mather, who shared his birthday month and shared the credit.

The richest year is fourth grade, 1968–69, with Miss Kathryn Ellis. Her class wrote lively news. Book reports launched paper rockets into “orbit,” and by December Shawn and Vicky Switzer had finished their second. In spring the reading project became a bulletin board “teeming” with green construction-paper worms, and Shawn's had three sections. When the class studied how to find north, he and Julie Irons brought in tree bark with lichens from the north side of a tree.

And in October 1968, a year after his brother Mike was wounded in Vietnam, Shawn brought Michael's combat helmet to school for the class to see, and a boomerang Mike had sent him. In the same paragraph, another child brought in a green tissue-paper “sea cucumber.”

Outside of class, his mother was den mother to Den 1 of the Cub Scouts. In February 1967 Shawn was in charge of the flag ceremony and also provided the treats, and the den hiked to the city lakes. In the summer of 1970, he took second place for boys 9 to 11 in the softball throw at the town games on the front page, behind John Fleming. The chalk drawing “Fun at the Pond” won him a third in the 1969 school art exhibit.

“Shawn Fitzpatrick brought his brother, Michael's combat helmet for us to see and also a boomerang his brother had sent him.”Fourth grade news, October 17, 1968

Were you in this class? Do you remember Miss Ellis, the rockets or the worms?

1951–1968Neil and Mary

Lulu Belle, Scotty and Five Children

Before Shawn was born, his parents were already regulars in the paper.

In March 1951, Neil and Mary Fitzpatrick played LuluBelle and Scotty, the famous radio couple, in the American Legion Auxiliary's home talent show, Uncle Ezra's Hayloft Jamboree, at the high school auditorium. Mary was president of the P-TA by 1953. And in the winter and spring of 1958, with four children at home and Denis still a baby, she published a run of poems in the paper, several of them on the front page.

Two are here. “The Answer” is about a new baby. “Seventeen” is the comic opposite, written in the voice of a smitten teenage girl, and it ends with a line any teenager would recognize.

Eight years later she was writing to the editor about what Weeping Water owed its young people. She tipped her hat to Larry and Lannie Hanson for opening the Alley Cat, where “youngsters from 12 to 20” could sit, talk and play their kind of music, and she added what the town still needed.

“I wish I were 60- or even dead; But I guess I'll just have to wait instead.”Mary Fitzpatrick, “Seventeen,” March 13, 1958

The children each got their moments. In a 1956 school program, Michael was the lame boy in the third grade's Pied Piper, and Brian was Geppetto in the second grade's Pinocchio. In 1959, kindergartner Sheila's painting “My Teddy Bear” was chosen for the all-state school exhibit, one of 474 from thousands submitted. In 1967, Denis and Rex Boyd drew straws for the job of copying out the fourth grade's letter to an Omaha pen pal. Rex won, or lost, depending on how you feel about ink. Remember Rex; he comes back later in this story.

Do you remember Neil and Mary, the Jamboree or the Alley Cat?

1952–1968St. Joseph, Missouri

The Grandfather Who Painted

Harrison and Mildred Hartley lived a hundred-odd miles down the Missouri, and the road between the two towns stayed busy.

Mary's father was a working artist, and the Republican was plainly proud to have him by marriage. It noted two of his paintings reproduced in color in Ford Times in 1952, his listing in Who's Who in American Artists in 1954, and the Pony Express pamphlet he designed and drew for the centennial rerun of the ride in 1960. In 1964 it ran a substantial story when he showed 52 paintings in St. Joseph, listing a career of more than 50 years as an art director and artist, with shows at the Nelson Gallery in Kansas City, Joslyn Memorial in Omaha and the National Arts Club in New York.

The clipping worth keeping, though, is from October 1959. On a weekend visit, Hartley sold three paintings to George Wiles and then, on Sunday afternoon, took his grandchildren out with him to paint the scenery around town. Shawn was nine months old and presumably stayed home. Six months earlier his sister's teddy bear had made the state show.

The grandparents came when babies arrived. Mildred stayed with Brian, Mike and Sheila when Denis was born. The children went the other way too: Mike and Brian by train for five days in 1960, Sheila home by bus in 1963, and in October 1968 the whole family on a Sunday, “Mike, Brian, Shelia, Shawn and Dennis.”

Does anyone in town still have a Harrison Hartley painting, perhaps one of George Wiles's three?

1964–1975The Martins

An Indefinite Visit

Art and Vi's grandchildren arrived from California and joined the school columns almost at once.

In August 1964, Vi Fitzpatrick came home from Redondo Beach with her daughter's two children, Debby and Roddie Martin, “for an indefinite visit with their grandparents.” Their mother, Shawn's aunt, followed; she appears as Marian, Marion and Maryon. For the next year the cousins were part of Weeping Water life.

Debbie wasted no time. That October, when her class wrote about the presidential race, she came down for Lyndon Johnson on the grounds of simple fairness: he had only had the job for a few months. By March she was the Brownie troop's news reporter, filing her own item and noting that the troop had sung Happy Birthday to her.

“How would you feel, if you were President for only a few short months and someone like Mr Goldwater came along and took your place?”Debbie Martin, October 8, 1964

In July 1965 Maryon, Rodney and Debra moved to Arvada, Colorado, with Vi and Neil going along to help. Ten years later, in January 1975, Marian moved back to Weeping Water to live with her widowed mother. Vi's obituary shows that Marian died before she did.

Did you go to school or Brownies with Debbie or Rodney Martin?

1966–1974The oldest brother

From Quang Tri to the Ballot

The war came into Shawn's fourth-grade classroom in the form of a helmet.

Mike Fitzpatrick enlisted in the Marines in 1966. In October 1967 the paper's front page reported that he had been wounded in action near Quang Tri, a little over a month after reaching Vietnam. The clipping sits behind a content note below; its last line says his condition was reported to be good.

The next spring he called his parents from Australia while on rest and recuperation leave. A week later the Around Town column added a detail that belongs in any family's stories: while staying in an Australian home, he had appeared on a one-man TV show.

Back in Nebraska he joined the Jaycees and, as their representative, repaired the foundation of the old Dr. Fate office for the Heritage House project in 1972. In 1974 he filed for the Legislature against the incumbent, Senator Calvin Carsten. The paper's verdict that November was not gentle: he “failed to carry one precinct.” He did take 990 votes in Cass County.

Do you remember Mike's campaign, or did you serve with him?

1972–1991Popping back up

Shawn and Dennis of Omaha

After the family moved on, the paper kept catching him on return trips, usually with a tool in his hand.

The paper does not record a moving day, and its hints point in slightly different directions. Neil is “of Millard” by 1971 and again in his father's 1973 obituary, and “Mrs. Mary Fitzpatrick and family of Omaha” appears in March 1972, yet Denis is still in Weeping Water school sports and honor rolls through 1974. What is clear is that by 1976 the paper calls them “Shawn and Dennis of Omaha.”

The town stayed close. In the summer of 1972 the paper's plea for help with Heritage House, the old Fate home and doctor's office, named the few who had worked on scraping and painting so far, and “Mike and Shawn Fitzpatrick” led the list. A month later, “Mike, Dennis and Shawn” dropped in on the Clifford Coopers. The Coopers had him back in April 1977, when he and Kit Cooper were their weekend visitors.

The best of these is August 1976. Vi, a widow for three years, was “surprised and delighted” one Sunday when her family turned up to shingle her roof: son Neil, a friend from Omaha, and grandsons Shawn, Denis, Mike and Brian, with Randy Beins and Mark Gwinn helping. They cleaned the yard too, and she served them a picnic dinner. The paper's spelling gave them “new singles.”

“Mrs. Vi Fitzpatrick was surprised and delighted Sunday when members of her family arrived to shingle the roof of her home.”Weeping Water Republican, August 12, 1976

In February 1989, a photo in the Plattsmouth Journal shows Margaret Boyd retiring from the city library. The last lines of the caption note a new library sign, “courtesy of Rex Boyd. Shawn Fitzpatrick put up the sign.” That may be the same Rex Boyd who drew the straw with Denis 22 years earlier. The caption doesn't say which Shawn Fitzpatrick it means, but the search found no other, so it is very likely him, at 30, back in town to hang a sign.

The last sighting is in March 1991. Violet Fitzpatrick died at 90, and the pallbearers were Brian, Dennis and Shawn Fitzpatrick, Mike Lozier (Sheila's husband), Farley Amick and Stanley Miller.

Have you got a later Shawn sighting, or know what the sign looked like?

Fall 1976Half real, half not

The Season That Wasn't

Shawn's class graduated in 1977, so its last football season was the fall of 1976. Here is what really happened to the Indians, and then a daydream about what might have happened had he stayed.

What really happened

Coach Jerry Park opened the year with a good feeling. His team had come through two bad seasons and had ended 1975 by beating Louisville. “They look so good, in fact, it scares me,” he told the paper.

For five weeks he looked right. After a 20–6 loss to Nebraska City Lourdes, the state's preseason favorite, the Indians won four straight. Then came four losses in a row, two of them by six points. They finished 4–5, scoring 140 points to their opponents' 192. (The nine reported scores add up to 193, one more than the paper's own season total.)

They were a running team, averaging 233.6 yards a game and passing for only 31.7. Rick Wilson led with 686 yards, Chester Taylor had 633, and Dave Schreiner scored 50 points. Wilson, Lloyd Philpot and Steve Mason made the Galaxy North all-conference team. The team's real trouble shows in the statistics: 20 lost fumbles and only 10 of 20 conversions.

The senior letter winners were Pat Brim, Mike Keckler, Lloyd Philpot, Dave Schreiner, Jeff Stander and Rick Wilson. Some of those names are in the earlier chapters of this page. A Michael Keckler was in Shawn's 1964 kindergarten class and took a third in the 1969 art exhibit, and a Richard Wilson was launching paper rockets beside him in fourth grade. Michael Spohr, who also shared those fourth-grade columns, appears to be the Mike Spohr listed as a student manager.

Imagined. None of this happened.

If He Had Stayed: The Legend of “Fishpatrick”

Everything in this box is invented for fun. The paper shows Shawn “of Omaha” by August 1976, and nothing in it says he played football that year. Only the “Actual” column below is real. Every real player's real record stands in the clippings above, and no real person's words are made up here.

Suppose the family never left, and the boy who brought a combat helmet to fourth grade grew up to be the best running back and middle linebacker in the Galaxy Conference. The team's real problems suggest what he might have changed. A back who holds on to the ball cuts into those 20 lost fumbles. Another 150-odd carries at seven yards each ease the load on a line that was already opening holes. And a linebacker helps a defense that gave up 197.8 rushing yards a game. Two of the five real losses were by six points, and those are the easiest ones to imagine turning around.

The sports page, of course, would have called him “Fishpatrick” from the first week, ten years after the original Charles. Headline writers are only human.

The imaginary stat line: 172 carries, 1,204 yards (7.0 a carry), 18 touchdowns, 104 tackles, 4 interceptions, 1 fumble at Homecoming. Unanimous all-conference on both sides of the ball. The imaginary Moccasin Prints column names him “Most Likely to Bring Cookies,” which the first grade could have told them in 1966.

The imaginary last scene: Wednesday night, October 27, 1976, Memorial Field. Vi Fitzpatrick, on a roof she can trust thanks to her grandsons, can nearly see the lights from home. Fourth and one. The handoff, the pile, the whistle, and the Indians are 7–2, the best team in town in years.

And then, as in the real world, everyone goes home for supper. In the real fall of 1976 he'd already been on that roof with a hammer in August, and that's the part the paper actually recorded.

What Else the Paper Printed

The same pages that printed the birthdays also printed the court docket and the hospital notes.

⚠️ Content note: this chapter covers injuries and accidents, including to children, a serious car crash and motorcycle crashes, a house fire, imprisonment abroad with reports of beatings, and court notices naming family members. Show this chapter

Accidents and illness

With five children and a country weekly watching, the injuries made the paper too. In 1956 Brian's bicycle hit a bump and left him with a slight concussion. Over the 1956 holidays Mike injured his left eye in a fall, and a week later the paper reported it “healing properly.” In November 1957 Sheila broke both bones in her wrist on the basement steps, and the next week Neil broke two ribs lifting pipe at work and went back to work the next morning. The headline was “Break ‘No Break’ for Fitzpatricks.”

In 1958 Mary came down with near pneumonia, and the Hartleys took baby Denis to St. Joseph while she recovered. That December a fire caught the kitchen curtains and walls, and she put it out herself. In 1960 Brian, alone on a Boy Scout survival hike, slipped, cut his head on a rock and needed stitches. The paper noted that this “gave it a genuine test.” In 1964 Denis had surgery in Lincoln; the paper doesn't say for what.

The worst of these was April 1966. Art Fitzpatrick, driving home from Manley, stopped at the highway 50 intersection and pulled out in front of another car, perhaps blinded by the setting sun. Vi was in critical condition with fractures and possible internal injuries, and both cars were “virtually demolished.” She came home a little over a week later and lived another 25 years. Neil had his own motorcycle accident and hospital stay in September 1968, and a card of thanks ran that month.

Brian in Mexico

The longest story is Brian's. On January 19, 1974, which happened to be Shawn's 15th birthday, Brian was arrested in Mexico and put in Lecumberri prison in Mexico City. A year later he still had not been tried. On January 2, 1975, the Republican announced a bake sale at the laundromat to pay for a lawyer, reporting that he had been severely beaten twice. The “Friends of Brian Fitzpatrick” raised $86.

That September the paper reported that his trial had been held on July 28, more than 18 months after his arrest and without him in the courtroom, and that the sentence was an $8,000 fine or three years. The American Legion post in Weeping Water set up a freedom fund for the honorably discharged Army veteran. Mary, identified as a laid-off Vickers employee, was the paper's source on the beatings. The two stories differ on the charge: one marijuana cigarette in the first, two among three men in the second.

The search did not find a report of his release. The next time he appears is August 12, 1976, on Vi's roof with his brothers.

The motorcycle, August 1976

Less than two weeks after the shingling, Brian's motorcycle left the 13-J Spur on a curve at about 3:30 in the morning, went through a ditch and hit a pole. He was not found until about 7:30. He had broken bones and possible internal injuries. The Rescue Squad's log and the news item give slightly different days, but they agree on the outcome: he regained consciousness that Monday and was recovering.

The docket

The rest is routine, and it's here because the paper printed everyone's. Brian paid $11 in 1969 for riding a motorcycle without lights and $33 in 1978 for drinking in public. Mike paid $60 for disorderly conduct and $48 for speeding, both in 1979. Shawn's one entry that the search found is a $51 speeding fine in June 1990, with an Omaha address. The notices give only a name and a town, so they are matched here by name.

The notice from Neil is different. It ran in December 1970 as a paid personal notice, with no explanation, and this page doesn't offer one.

Know how Brian's story ended, or have a correction to any of this?

Sources & Method

This page was built from the full-text OCR of 39,560 pages of the Weeping Water Republican, searching for Fitzpatrick, Shawn, Hartley and Martin with patterns that tolerate bad scans (“Fitz­ patrick,” “FitZpatrick,” “F'tzpatrick”). That found about 11,000 hits, sorted by first name and read in context. Pages of the Plattsmouth Journal (1986–2000) were searched through the library's online archive. Other Shawns and other Fitzpatricks in the area were set aside.

Every clipping here was read from the page image and transcribed by hand, because the OCR often runs neighboring columns together. The images were cleaned to flatten uneven lighting and restore a newsprint tone, not retouched. The narrative's connections, such as the Rex Boyd of 1967 and 1989, are inferences and are marked as such.

“The Season That Wasn't” is the one place on this page with invented material. It sits in a box labeled “Imagined”; its clippings and the “Actual” column are real, and the rest is a daydream.

This is a selection, not a complete record. Court notices and significant medical items sit behind content notes in “What Else the Paper Printed.” Passing colds, chicken pox and the like were left out, and so were other people's names in the same docket columns where a clean crop allowed it.
